Which Trading Sayings Hold Up? A Scorecard of 34 Claims

Of 34 popular trading sayings tested on up to ten markets (2010-2026, before costs), 1 holds, 11 partly hold, 19 do not hold and 3 lack data; most of the survivors describe risk, not a way to predict returns.

Claim CheckerTrading SayingsMultiple TestingScorecard

What was tested

The site's claim checker turns 34 trading sayings into mechanical tests: golden crosses, the 200-day average, buying dips, RSI levels, calendar effects, dollar-cost averaging, gold as a hedge, bitcoin as digital gold, and so on. Each is run on up to ten markets: US stocks, gold, silver, oil, natural gas, broad commodities, agriculture, EUR/USD, bitcoin and ether, using daily data from 2010 where available (bitcoin and ether start later) up to 2026-11-11.

All results are before trading costs, and cash earns nothing. A market counts as a clear and stable pass only if the effect is statistically visible (|t| of at least 2, with at least 20 independent events) and points the claimed way in both halves of its history. A claim "holds" if at least 60% of its usable markets pass, with few contradictions.

The scorecard

  • Holds: 1 of 34 (3%)
  • Partly holds: 11 of 34 (32%)
  • Does not hold: 19 of 34 (56%)
  • Not enough data: 3 of 34 (9%)

By type of test: all 8 calendar sayings fail or lack data (7 do not hold, 1 has too few observations). Five of the 6 rule backtests are "partly" (moving averages, MACD, Turtle breakouts, trend following), against 3 of 10 event studies and 2 of 6 relationship claims. In 25 of 34 claims no market was both clear and stable; in 9 at least one was.

The three "not enough data" claims are the bitcoin four-year halving cycle (at most 3 independent events), "blood in the streets" (at most 9 events in any market) and the Uptober crypto effect (at most 13 observations).

The best performers

The only claim that holds is "stairs up, elevator down": daily returns are negatively skewed. Over 2010-01-01 to 2026-11-11 (4,240 daily returns per market) it passed clearly in US stocks (skewness -0.34), gold (-0.54), silver (-1.14) and broad commodities (-0.52). Agriculture pointed the same way (-0.08), and EUR/USD showed none (0.00). Notably it is a statement about the shape of returns, not a forecast.

Next come the trend rules. The 200-day average rule passed clearly in 3 of 10 markets (median maximum drawdown -51% against -71% for buy-and-hold); the 252-day time-series momentum rule ("the trend is your friend") beat buy-and-hold on Sharpe in 6 of 10 and passed clearly in natural gas, broad commodities and bitcoin. MACD and Turtle breakouts passed in 2 markets each. "Time in the market beats timing" is partly supported: missing the best 10 days cost a median 36% of total growth across five markets.

The worst performers

Dollar-cost averaging against lump sum is the clearest miss. Over 12-month windows from 2010-01-01 to 2026-11-11, DCA ended ahead on a median 50% of start dates, and in 3 of 10 markets the lump sum clearly won. "Bitcoin is digital gold" is the reverse of the claim: on the 99 days (2014-09-18 to 2026-11-11) when US stocks fell 2% or more, bitcoin returned -3.07% on average against +0.25% on all days (t = -4.6).

The "bounce after three down days" was contradicted in 2 markets and confirmed in none. Calendar sayings were flat: May to October in US stocks returned +1.0% a month against +1.4% for the other months (102 months, t = -0.7). The Monday effect, the Santa Claus rally and turn-of-the-month all failed on US stocks, which is the only market their verdicts rest on.

What the survivors have in common

Twelve claims hold or partly hold. Sorted by what they test, 5 are trend rules (golden cross, 200-day, trend following, MACD, Turtle), 4 describe how returns or markets behave (stairs up, time in the market, oil and stocks, correlations in a crisis) and 3 are "what happens next" event studies (buy the dip, new highs, falling knife).

Four of the five trend rules are judged on risk-adjusted terms or drawdown, not raw return. For the 200-day rule the clear passes were natural gas, agriculture and EUR/USD, where buy-and-hold made -23.2%, 0.4% and -1.4% a year. The three event studies each have at most 1 clear market. So the sayings that survive mostly say something about risk or structure; none gives a clear, repeated way to forecast returns.

Some will look good by chance

With 34 claims and up to ten markets each, some results will look good by luck. Of the 71 market-level t-tests (the main condition of each event study plus the calendar tests), 5 (7.0%) reached |t| of at least 2 with enough events, and only 2 (2.8%) did so in the claimed direction. A threshold of 2 is passed by roughly 4.6% of pure-noise tests, which would be about 3.2 of 71, or 1.6 in the claimed direction. The count of significant results is close to what luck alone could give.

That is why the checker adds the stability test across two halves and requires a majority of markets, and why a single clear market, such as broad commodities in the buy-the-dip test, should be read as a lead rather than a finding.

How to read a verdict

  • Sample size. 15 of 34 verdicts rest on a single market (most calendar and relationship claims), and event studies need at least 10 events per market to be usable. A market with 7 golden crosses or 38 dips is a small sample.
  • Before costs. Cash earns nothing and no spread or commission is charged, so rules that trade often, such as three down days, look better here than they would in practice.
  • What "partly" means. Of 11 partly verdicts, 5 are direction only (most markets point the right way, none is clear), 4 are "some markets" (a few clear passes) and 2 are weak effects.
  • The period. 2010-2026 includes long rises in US stocks, gold and crypto, and the results are only as general as that history.

Takeaways

  • 1 of 34 sayings holds and 19 do not; 12 hold at least in part, mostly trend rules and facts about risk.
  • No calendar saying survived, and none of the "what happens next" sayings was clear in more than 1 market.
  • Judge a saying by its sample size, its costs and how many others were tested alongside it, not by one good-looking number.

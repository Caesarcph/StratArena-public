How the Arena Score Ranks Trading Strategies (and What It Misses)

The Arena Score ranks each strategy by percentile within its own market and window, using five weighted components. On US stocks over five years it moves 7 of 59 strategies by more than 10 places compared with ranking by return, but only 1 compared with ranking by Sharpe ratio, and none of 525 five-year results clears a deflated Sharpe ratio of 0.95.

A rank among peers, not a grade

The Arena Score is calculated separately for every market and every time window (1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, 3 years, 5 years and the full history). Within one market and window, each strategy is compared with all the others. Each metric is turned into a percentile from 0 to 100, with the lowest value at 0, the highest at 100 and ties sharing their average rank.

Two consequences follow. A score says where a strategy stands among its peers, not how good it is in absolute terms. And a strategy that was in the market, meaning it had a non-zero return, on fewer than 5% of days in that window is not ranked at all and gets a score of 0. On US stocks over five years, two strategies were left out for that reason.

The five components

The score is a weighted average of five parts:

  • Return quality, 30%: the average of the percentiles for the Sharpe ratio and the Sortino ratio. Both use daily returns, annualised, with a risk-free rate of zero; Sortino divides by the deviation of losing days only.
  • Drawdown control, 25%: the average of the percentiles for the Calmar ratio (annual return divided by the maximum drawdown) and the Ulcer index (the root-mean-square drawdown, where lower is better).
  • Consistency, 20%: the average of the percentiles for the share of calendar months with a gain and the share of days spent below a previous equity peak (lower is better).
  • Robustness, 15%: the percentile of the worst Sharpe ratio among the 1-year, 3-year, 5-year and full-history windows. The same number is used whichever window is being ranked.
  • Significance, 10%: the probabilistic Sharpe ratio times 100. This is the only part that is not a percentile.

If a part cannot be computed, for example because a window is too short, the weights of the remaining parts are rescaled. There are no caps and no explicit penalties apart from the 5% rule, and trading costs are not in the score. We recomputed the score from the stored metrics: the result matches exactly for US stocks over five years and is within 0.93 points in all 40 cohorts of the 1-, 3- and 5-year and full-history windows.

The older formula that still picks parameters

An earlier linear score is still in the code: 40 times Sharpe, plus 20 times Sortino, plus 10 times Calmar, plus 100 times the annual return, minus 50 times the absolute maximum drawdown, clipped to the range 0 to 100. It is stored as the version-1 score, and it is the formula the walk-forward step uses to choose parameters. Each fold picks the setting with the best old-style score over the previous 3 years and trades it for the next 6 months.

For ranking, the two versions agree closely: the rank correlation between them on US stocks over five years is 0.96. They disagree at the top. The older formula puts Cross-Asset Momentum (MACRO-01) first; the current one puts Connors RSI (MR-16) first.

Score, return and Sharpe compared on US stocks

We ranked the 59 US stock strategies that the score ranks over the last five years, before trading costs.

  • By Arena Score: Connors RSI (MR-16), Trend + Macro Filter (EVENT-01), Cross-Asset Momentum (MACRO-01), Multi-Factor Composite (FACTOR-05), Z-Score Mean Reversion (MR-06).
  • By annual return: Cross-Asset Momentum (11.3%), Multi-Factor Composite (10.6%), Trend + Macro Filter (10.0%), Triple Moving Average (TREND-13, 10.0%), Dual Moving Average (TREND-05, 9.6%).
  • By Sharpe ratio: Cross-Asset Momentum (1.06), Connors RSI (1.03), Trend + Macro Filter (0.93), Z-Score Mean Reversion (0.89), Accelerating Momentum (MOM-10, 0.83).

The top five by score shares 3 names with the top five by return and 4 with the top five by Sharpe. Seven strategies change rank by more than 10 places between score and return; only one does between score and Sharpe. Across all ten markets over five years the counts are 41 of 525 against return and 8 of 525 against Sharpe. The first-place strategy by score is also first by return in 5 of 10 markets and first by Sharpe in 3.

The winner on score, Connors RSI, was invested on 12% of days, earned 7.9% a year (13th by return) with a Sharpe ratio of 1.03 and a maximum drawdown of -7.6%. RSI Mean Reversion (MR-01) ranks 27th by return but 9th by score: 5.2% a year, a -7.2% drawdown and 5.9% of days invested. In this cohort the score behaves like a Sharpe ranking with extra weight on drawdown and consistency, which lifts low-exposure strategies such as Connors RSI and RSI Mean Reversion.

How many results clear a luck threshold

The probabilistic Sharpe ratio (PSR) is the probability that the true Sharpe ratio is above zero, adjusted for the skew and kurtosis of the returns. The deflated Sharpe ratio (DSR) raises the bar: it tests the Sharpe ratio against the best result expected by chance from N skill-less strategies, where N is the number of strategies compared in the same market and window (in the code, the cohort size).

Over five years, 58 of the 525 ranked strategy and market results (11%) have a PSR above 0.95. None has a DSR above 0.95, and 61 have a DSR above 0.5. The median PSR is 0.77 and the median DSR 0.18. The highest DSR is Vortex Indicator (TREND-20) on EUR/USD at 0.86. Over the full history, 166 results (32%) have a PSR above 0.95 and exactly one has a DSR above 0.95: Parabolic SAR (SAR-01) on EUR/USD, at 0.99. Of the 44 five-year results that scored 80 or more, none clears 0.95 on DSR.

On US stocks the five highest-scoring strategies all have a PSR above 0.95 and none has a DSR above 0.95, and the same is true of the top five by return and by Sharpe. Treating every strategy in the cohort as an independent trial is a strict assumption when many are variants of one idea, so the DSR may be too severe; but a high score is clearly not evidence of skill.

What the score cannot tell you

  • It is relative. On natural gas over five years only 3 of 50 ranked strategies had a positive annual return. The top score, 86.02, went to RSI Mean Reversion (MR-01), which earned 2.7% a year with a -46.3% drawdown; holding lost 31.6% a year with a -93.3% drawdown.
  • It depends on the window. On US stocks, Trend + Macro Filter is second over five years and 27th over the full history; Z-Score Mean Reversion falls from fifth to 38th. Only 3 of the top 5 stay in the top 10 (rank correlation between the two windows: 0.73).
  • It ignores costs. At the site's assumption of 10 basis points per unit of turnover, Connors RSI would give up 3.0% a year on US stocks against its 7.9% return, and Cross-Asset Momentum, with 1,253 trades (entries plus exits), 2.7% against 11.3%.
  • It is not significance. Significance is only 10% of the weight.

Takeaways

  • The Arena Score is a weighted percentile rank: 30% quality, 25% drawdown control, 20% consistency, 15% robustness, 10% significance, within one market and window.
  • It is close to a Sharpe ranking (1 of 59 strategies moves by more than 10 places) and far from a return ranking (7 of 59).
  • Use it to shortlist, then check the deflated Sharpe ratio, the time in market, the trade count and a second window.
  • With a luck-adjusted test, almost nothing clears the bar over five years: none of 525 results does.

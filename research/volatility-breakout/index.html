Does a Volatility Breakout Strategy Work? A Ten-Market Test

Volatility Breakout (VOL-01) buys after an unusually large up day and sells at the first down day, so it was invested on only 14% of days on US stocks over five years: 1.1% a year against 11.9% for holding, with a 10.1% maximum drawdown instead of 24.5%. Keltner Channel Breakout (KC-01) stayed in the market about twice as long and traded far less.

What the rule does, from the code

Volatility Breakout (VOL-01) looks at one number each day: the close-to-close return. It also computes the standard deviation of the last 20 daily returns. If today's return is above 1.5 times that standard deviation, the strategy is long tomorrow. If it is not, and today's return is negative, the strategy is flat. Otherwise it keeps yesterday's position.

That is the whole rule. The strategy page mentions an ATR threshold, smaller positions in high volatility and a stop after a 10% drawdown; the code has none of them. It uses the standard deviation of returns, a fixed 100% position and no stop. It also exposes no parameters, so the walk-forward step used on the other strategies has nothing to choose here: every fold of the log on all ten markets uses the same empty configuration. All figures below are before trading costs, with a position decided at the close of day t earning the return of day t+1.

How it actually behaves

Because any down day switches the strategy off, it is almost never invested. Averaged over the ten markets for the full history, it held a position on 12.4% of days. The average holding period was 2.1 days, 47% of all holds lasted a single day and the longest was 11 days.

The implied bet is that a big up day is followed by another non-negative day. The data are mixed. On US stocks there were 320 trigger days, and the next day was up 51.9% of the time, against 55.2% for all days; the average next-day return was -0.10%, against +0.06% for all days. On silver the average next day after a trigger was +0.29% (all days +0.04%), on oil +0.25% (all days +0.01%), and on natural gas -0.34% (all days -0.07%). Follow-through depends on the market, and it was not there on US stocks.

Results across ten markets

Over the five years to 11 November 2026, and before costs, Volatility Breakout had a positive Sharpe ratio on 8 of 10 markets but beat the Sharpe ratio of simply holding on only two:

  • Silver: 9.4% a year, Sharpe 0.78, maximum drawdown -15.1%, against 14.8%, 0.56 and -57.8% for holding.
  • Ether: 7.1% a year, Sharpe 0.34, maximum drawdown -36.6%, while holding lost 15.4% a year with a -79.0% drawdown.
  • US stocks: 1.1% a year, Sharpe 0.21, drawdown -10.1%, against 11.9%, 0.74 and -24.5%.
  • Natural gas: -21.7% a year, Sharpe -0.79, drawdown -80.0%, although holding did worse (-31.6% a year).

The drawdown was smaller than holding's on all 10 markets, but that is largely arithmetic: a strategy that is invested one day in eight cannot lose much in a fall. Over the full period (since January 2010 for US stocks, September 2014 for bitcoin and November 2017 for ether) it beat holding's Sharpe on 5 of 10 markets. Ether returned 22.8% a year with a -37.8% drawdown, level with holding's 22.9% but with a -94.0% drawdown for the holder; bitcoin returned 17.1% a year against 52.0% for holding.

A bad stretch, and a good one

The worst stretch was natural gas. From the peak on 18 April 2022 to the trough on 11 August 2026 the strategy fell 80.0%; holding the fund over the same dates fell 90.7%. It was invested on only 101 of those 1,083 trading days, consistent with an average next-day return of -0.34% after a trigger on this market.

The best stretch for its drawdown profile was 2022 (3 January to 12 October). US stocks fell 24.5% and Volatility Breakout gained 0.3%. On bitcoin it lost 16.4% while holding lost 58.8%, and on ether it gained 15.6% while holding lost 65.6%. The same flatness cost it in rising markets: on oil it made 5.3% while holding made 29.4%, and on natural gas 8.5% against 73.1%.

Keltner Channel Breakout, the slower relative

Keltner Channel Breakout (KC-01) works on a different timescale. It builds a channel from an exponential moving average of the close, plus and minus 2.0 times the average true range. It goes long when the close is above the upper band and exits when the close falls below the middle line (or below the lower band). It is long only. In the walk-forward test the moving-average and ATR lengths were chosen from 16, 20 and 24 days; the multiplier stayed at 2.0.

The two behave very differently. Averaged over the ten markets, KC-01 was invested 26% of days over five years against 12% for VOL-01, and made 44 trades per market against 166 (the site counts each entry and each exit as a trade). On Sharpe ratio the quick rule was ahead on 6 of 10 markets over five years, and the slow one on 6 of 10 over the full history (VOL-01 led on 4). KC-01 beat holding's Sharpe on 7 markets over the full period, against 5 for VOL-01.

On bitcoin KC-01 made 14.6% a year over five years, with a -32.9% drawdown, against 2.6% and -75.9% for holding; over the full period it made 53.8% a year with a Sharpe ratio of 1.13 and a -45.5% drawdown. On US stocks it made 4.5% a year with an 8.5% drawdown (Sharpe 0.69). It is not a safe harbour: its drawdown was not smaller than holding's on oil, broad commodities or agriculture, and in 2022 it lost 24.2% on natural gas while holding gained 73.1%.

What the numbers cannot tell you

The most important caveat is cost. The site's cost-drag estimate assumes 10 basis points per unit of turnover. On US stocks that comes to 3.1% a year for VOL-01, more than its 1.1% return, against 0.7% for KC-01. A strategy that makes 166 trades per market in five years, on average, needs a large edge per trade, and these results do not include that cost.

Statistical confidence is also thin. The probabilistic Sharpe ratio (the chance that the true Sharpe is above zero) was 0.97 for VOL-01 on silver and 0.83 on ether. After adjusting for how many strategies the site compares, the deflated Sharpe ratio fell to 0.55 and 0.27. The best-looking results are small samples of a strategy that is mostly in cash, and a different five-year window could rank the markets differently.

Takeaways

  • Volatility Breakout (VOL-01) is a one-to-two-day bet on follow-through after a big up day; it was invested on about 12% of days and held 2.1 days on average.
  • Its low drawdown mostly reflects being out of the market, not skill at avoiding losses: it also gave up the strong rallies of 2022.
  • Follow-through after a big up day is market-specific: positive on silver and oil, negative on US stocks and natural gas.
  • Keltner Channel Breakout (KC-01) is slower, trades about a quarter as often and did better on bitcoin, but its drawdown protection is less consistent.
  • Trading costs would erase much of the quicker rule's return; check the cost assumption before drawing conclusions.

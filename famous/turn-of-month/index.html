Turn-of-the-Month Effect: does it still work? Replicated through 2026

We re-ran it on S&P 500 from 2010-01-04 to 2026-11-12 (16.9 years, before costs): +3.3% a year, Sharpe 0.49, worst drawdown -13.8%. The benchmark, S&P 500 buy-and-hold, did +13.9% a year (Sharpe 0.85, worst drawdown -33.7%).

Verdict: Worse risk-adjusted return than its benchmark over this period. Historical, hypothetical results before costs, taxes and fees. Not investment advice.

Rule as published Rule as published

This replica applies the published rule as written, on the instrument we have. It is still a backtest on ETF data, before costs.

What is lost

  • The exact window follows our reading of the paper and may differ by a day.
  • SPY instead of the Dow Jones index over 90 years.

Where it comes from

Attributed toJosef Lakonishok & Edwin Smidt
SourceAre Seasonal Anomalies Real? A Ninety-Year Perspective, Review of Financial Studies (1988)

A founding academic paper on calendar anomalies, now a standard reference in seasonality research.

Rules, weights and thresholds are given as commonly cited. We have not re-checked them against the primary text.

The original rules

Using 90 years of Dow data, nearly all price gains came in the four trading days around the turn of the month, from the last trading day to the third trading day of the new month.

How we replicate it

What we changed

SPY only. We hold the window from the close before the last trading day through the third trading day of the next month, so the last day's return is captured; cash otherwise.

The exact rule we run

Buy 100% SPY at the close of the second-to-last trading day of each month; sell to cash at the close of the 3rd trading day of the next month. Otherwise hold cash.

Conventions: Position decided at a close earns the next day's return; no look-ahead; before costs; cash earns 0%; starts 2010-01-04, the first day a signal exists; every market has an equal slot of equity; indicators use each market's own trading days.
